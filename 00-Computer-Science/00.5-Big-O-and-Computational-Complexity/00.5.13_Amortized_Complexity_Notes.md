# Module 00.5.13 — Amortized Complexity

## Motivation
Python lists are dynamic arrays. Most `append()` operations fit into allocated capacity and are inexpensive. Occasionally, the list may need a larger allocation and existing elements may need to be copied, making that particular append `O(n)`.

```python
numbers = []
for i in range(1000):
    numbers.append(i)
```

Despite occasional expensive resizes, Python list append has **amortized `O(1)` time** under the usual dynamic-array model.

## Meaning
Amortized analysis studies the total cost of a sequence of operations and establishes a bound on the cost per operation across that sequence.

For `n` appends:
- Total work is `O(n)` under the usual dynamic-array model.
- Amortized cost per append is `O(1)`.
- An individual append that triggers resizing can take `O(n)`.

This does not mean every append is constant-time. It means total work across the sequence grows linearly, so the amortized cost per operation is constant.

## Amortized vs. average-case

| Analysis | Main question |
|---|---|
| **Average-case** | Expected cost under a specified probability distribution over inputs or cases |
| **Amortized** | Bound on cost per operation across a sequence |

Amortized analysis does not require random inputs. It can bound a sequence even when some operations are unusually expensive.

## AI engineering connection
Appending chunks or results to a Python list is generally efficient. More important questions may be how much work chunking requires, how many objects are created, and how much memory the collection consumes.

## Key takeaways
- Amortized `O(1)` does not mean every individual operation is `O(1)`.
- Occasional expensive operations are spread across a sequence.
- Amortized analysis differs from average-case analysis.
